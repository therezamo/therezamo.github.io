Macroeconomic stability is often discussed through big numbers: inflation, unemployment, GDP growth, and interest rates. But those numbers are outcomes of millions of individual decisions. Households decide how much to consume today or save for tomorrow. Firms decide whether to invest, hire, or wait. Central banks make announcements that influence what people expect the future to look like.

The 2026 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded to Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford for their work on the microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation, points to a powerful idea: to understand the economy as a whole, we need to understand how individual choices and expectations fit together.

My takeaway from their work is that economic stabilisation is not simply a matter of adjusting a policy rate until the right numbers appear. It is about shaping the environment in which households and firms make decisions—and recognising the limits of what policymakers can achieve.

## Robert Hall: Consumption is about the future, not just today

In his influential 1978 paper, “Stochastic Implications of the Life Cycle–Permanent Income Hypothesis: Theory and Evidence,” Robert E. Hall examined a central question: if people plan consumption based on the income they expect over their lifetime, what should we observe when new information arrives?

The permanent-income hypothesis, associated with Milton Friedman, argues that households do not base consumption solely on current income. They also consider longer-term resources and expectations. A temporary pay rise may lead to some extra spending, while a lasting change in expected income can have a much larger effect.

Hall developed a testable implication of this logic. Under a set of assumptions, if consumers already use all available information efficiently, changes in consumption should be difficult to predict using information they already knew. New information can change consumption; information that is already reflected in people’s plans should not systematically do so again. Hall’s paper showed how this insight could be expressed as a random-walk result for consumption, with qualifications arising from the model’s assumptions (Hall, 1978).

The intuition matters beyond the technical result. A government cannot reliably predict how much households will spend simply by looking at current income. People may save a temporary windfall, increase spending when they expect a durable improvement in their prospects, or cut back when they fear future instability.

There is an important caveat: this is not a claim that all consumers behave identically or that consumption is literally impossible to forecast in every circumstance. Credit constraints, uncertainty, unequal access to financial markets, and differences between households can all weaken the simplest version of the model. Hall’s contribution was to turn a broad theory of forward-looking consumption into a sharp empirical proposition—not to eliminate the complexity of real-world behaviour.

For policy, the lesson is that the timing and perceived durability of a change matter. A transfer believed to be temporary may have a different effect from a credible long-term increase in disposable resources. The response depends not only on how much money arrives, but also on what households think it means for their future.

## Michael Woodford: Monetary policy works through expectations

Michael Woodford’s *Interest and Prices: Foundations of a Theory of Monetary Policy* (2003) helped establish a modern framework for analysing monetary policy through forward-looking behaviour, price-setting decisions, and expectations. In this approach, monetary policy is not just a mechanical relationship between today’s interest rate and today’s inflation. Its effects depend partly on how policy changes expectations about future economic conditions.

Consider a central bank that announces a change in its policy stance. Businesses setting prices and wages, investors allocating capital, and households deciding whether to borrow or save all have to form expectations about what comes next. If people believe the central bank’s commitment, its announcement can influence current decisions before the full effects of the policy rate work through the economy.

This is why credibility matters. A central bank may have a technically sophisticated model, but if its announcements are inconsistent or its commitment is not believed, the same policy action can have a different effect. Clear communication and a coherent policy framework can help anchor expectations; they cannot guarantee that every shock will be offset.

Woodford’s framework also offers a way to think about why policy rules and commitments matter. Policymakers face a temptation to respond to short-term pressures in ways that undermine their longer-term objectives. If households and firms anticipate that inconsistency, they may adjust their behaviour accordingly. A credible strategy can make policy more effective because expectations respond to the strategy, not merely to the latest rate decision.

In my view, this is one of the most useful shifts in how we think about central banking: communication is not decoration around the “real” policy. It is part of the mechanism through which policy operates.

## Why interest rates cannot solve every problem

The power of monetary policy should not be confused with unlimited control. Woodford’s later work has explicitly examined the limits of stabilisation through interest-rate policy. In “Effective Demand Failures and the Limits of Monetary Stabilization Policy,” he argues that some disruptions—particularly those involving unusual failures of demand and constraints on conventional policy—may not be adequately addressed by rate cuts alone (Woodford, 2022).

When policy rates are already very low, a central bank may have limited room to reduce them further. Even when rates can be cut, households and firms may remain unwilling or unable to spend because they face uncertainty, damaged balance sheets, or a lack of viable investment opportunities. In such cases, fiscal measures can play a distinct role. Transfers or public spending may reach the groups or activities where demand is most impaired, although their effects depend on design, timing, financing, and the state of the economy.

This does not mean fiscal policy is always superior, or that monetary policy becomes irrelevant. It means the source of the problem matters. A broad inflationary shock, a collapse in private demand, a disruption to supply, and a financial crisis are not interchangeable events. Treating them as if they were the same problem can lead to blunt or poorly timed responses.

The implication I draw is straightforward: stabilisation requires diagnosis before intervention. Policymakers need to ask what is preventing households and firms from acting, which constraints are binding, and which tools can realistically change those conditions.

## The limits of models—and the importance of institutions

Micro-founded models improve macroeconomics because they make assumptions about individual behaviour explicit. They force economists to explain why consumption, investment, prices, and expectations respond as they do, rather than relying only on correlations in aggregate data.

But a model is still a simplified representation. Results depend on assumptions about information, market access, how quickly prices adjust, and how people form expectations. An elegant model can clarify a mechanism while leaving important parts of the real economy outside its frame.

That is especially relevant when households face very different constraints. A household with savings and access to credit may respond to an interest-rate change differently from one living paycheque to paycheque. A large company with several financing options may react differently from a small firm whose cash flow is already under pressure. Aggregate indicators can conceal those differences.

Institutions matter, too. The credibility of a central bank, the reliability of public communication, the quality of fiscal administration, and the stability of the financial system all affect whether a policy commitment translates into real decisions. Expectations are shaped not only by what officials say, but also by what institutions have demonstrated they can do.

For these reasons, I would not interpret the micro-foundations approach as a promise that policymakers can fine-tune the economy with precision. Its greater value is that it helps identify the channels through which policy works, the assumptions required for it to work, and the circumstances in which those assumptions may fail.

## My view: stabilisation is the management of expectations and constraints

The connection between Hall and Woodford is especially compelling. Hall shows why consumption depends on what people learn about their future resources, not merely on the income they receive today. Woodford shows how policy affects the economy partly by changing what people expect about future conditions and the future policy path.

Together, these ideas suggest that a stabilisation strategy must be judged on more than its immediate headline effect. Does it change expectations in a credible way? Does it address the constraint actually holding spending or investment back? Does it account for the fact that households and firms have different resources and different degrees of flexibility? And can institutions deliver what they promise?

This also means there is no universal policy lever. Interest rates can influence financing conditions and expectations, but they cannot repair every balance sheet or remove every source of uncertainty. Fiscal support can sustain demand in some circumstances, but it is not costless and must be designed carefully. Communication can strengthen credibility, but words lose force when actions repeatedly contradict them.

The broader lesson is not that macroeconomic stabilisation is impossible. It is that effective stabilisation requires a realistic account of how people make decisions—and a clear-eyed view of the limits of each policy instrument.

## Conclusion

Hall and Woodford help move macroeconomics away from treating the economy as a machine controlled by a few aggregate dials. Consumption reflects expectations about lifetime resources; monetary policy works partly through beliefs about the future; and the success of stabilisation depends on the interaction between individual choices, constraints, and credible institutions.

My interpretation is that good economic policy is less about promising control and more about improving the conditions under which people can plan. Policymakers are most effective when they understand the mechanism behind a shock, choose tools suited to that mechanism, and communicate a strategy that people have reason to believe.

That is a demanding standard. It is also a more useful one than assuming that a change in one policy rate can solve every macroeconomic problem.
